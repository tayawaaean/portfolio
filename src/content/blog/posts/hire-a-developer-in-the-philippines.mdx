## Why the Philippines specifically

Founders looking to hire outside the US or Canada often find the Philippines through word of mouth, then struggle to separate the real advantages from the pitch of staffing agencies competing for their business. This guide covers what actually changes when you hire from the Philippines and how to get the setup right.

Several factors make the Philippines a strong market for web development talent, not just a cost-efficiency play.

**English as a working language.** English is an official language in the Philippines and the medium of instruction in most university IT and computer science programs. Code comments, pull request descriptions, Slack conversations, and technical discussions happen in clear, colloquial English — reducing the kind of interpretation overhead that compounds across hundreds of async exchanges.

**Time zone overlap with the US and Canada.** Philippine Standard Time is UTC+8. Most Filipino developers who work with North American clients shift their hours to create real overlap: mid-to-late evening in Manila lands in early morning on the US West Coast and mid-morning on the East Coast. Many experienced developers in this market have already solved the logistics, so time zone is a scheduling conversation, not a structural barrier.

**A track record of production web development for international clients.** Filipino engineers have delivered SaaS platforms, marketplaces, e-commerce systems, and data applications for international companies for well over a decade. The [AREC GIS platform](/portfolio/arec-gis-platform) — a geospatial renewable energy tracking system adopted by the Philippine Department of Energy to monitor installations nationwide — is one example of what production-grade, nationally deployed software built by Filipino engineers looks like.

**Cost.** Rates are substantially lower than equivalent US or Canadian market rates. Worth noting, but anchoring a hiring decision on cost is how founders end up with the wrong person at a low rate — more expensive than the right person at a higher one.

## What to actually evaluate

The standard developer vetting criteria — deployed work you can click, the ability to explain architectural decisions in plain language, clear IP ownership terms — apply here exactly as they do to any developer hire. What changes when you hire offshore from the Philippines is the engagement structure and the operational setup. These are where founders consistently underinvest.

**Deployed, live work.** A portfolio of screenshots and private repositories tells you very little. Ask for a link you can open and use. If a candidate cannot show you anything public or share a read-only demo environment, treat that as a meaningful signal about how they approach their work.

**End-to-end ownership.** A strong developer should be able to explain — in plain language — how they designed the database, what API decisions they made and why, how the deployment works, and what they would change if they did it again. If they can only speak to a specific slice of a larger team's work, that context matters before you hand them a solo project.

**Communication under ambiguity.** Async communication is where offshore arrangements succeed or fail. Send a written message that requires some judgment — something without an obvious right answer — and pay attention to whether they ask clarifying questions or just interpret and proceed. The ability to surface ambiguity rather than paper over it is a senior-engineer trait, and it is exactly what you need from someone working largely independently.

**IP and access terms.** You should own the repository, the database, the production cloud accounts, the domain, and every third-party service credential created for the project. A good developer will agree to this immediately — many will insist on it because it keeps the relationship clean and offboarding simple. Anyone who hedges on ownership is a significant warning sign.

## Engagement models

**Direct freelance or contractor.** You engage a specific individual, pay them directly, and work with them as an independent contractor — the most flexible arrangement with the clearest line of accountability. This works well for a defined project, an ongoing retainer, or a full-time offshore team member you want fully integrated into your workflow.

**Staffing agency.** An agency assigns a developer or team to your project and handles payroll, HR, and staffing continuity. The structural risk: agencies can rotate staff without notice, assign junior developers without disclosing it, and insert account managers between you and the actual work. If you go this route, negotiate the right to interview and approve the specific developer before work starts, and get that in writing.

**Employer of record (EOR).** You hire a specific developer full-time through a third-party EOR that handles Philippine payroll, benefits, and compliance on your behalf. Full-time dedication and team integration without incorporating in the Philippines. The right structure once you have found someone you want long-term — it costs more than a contractor arrangement and less than equivalent US employment.

For most early-stage builds, a direct freelance engagement with a single accountable developer — someone in your Slack, your code reviews, your standups — outperforms an agency arrangement. The value of a strong [full-stack web development](/services/full-stack-web-development) engagement is precisely that single-owner accountability: one developer who can hold the whole system in their head and make coherent decisions across the stack without waiting on handoffs.

## Setting up the relationship

**Clarify ownership before any code is written.** Agree in writing on who owns the repository, the database, the cloud accounts, the domain, and every third-party service credential. Define what offboarding looks like if you need to transition to another developer. A developer who handles this cleanly is almost always easier to work with in every other respect too.

**Agree on overlapping working hours.** Define when you will both be reachable for calls and reviews, and commit to it. Even two or three hours of reliable daily overlap makes async collaboration substantially smoother. Formalizing the window before work starts prevents "flexible hours" from becoming "no hours you can reliably reach them."

**Start with a paid trial task.** A self-contained piece of real work — paid at their normal rate — tells you far more than any portfolio review. Watch how they handle ambiguity: do they ask clarifying questions or interpret and proceed? Is what they deliver something you could actually deploy? Paying properly for a trial also signals you take the engagement seriously, which attracts better candidates.

**Run daily written updates.** A short end-of-day summary — what was completed, what is next, what is blocked — keeps you informed without constant synchronous contact. Async updates you can read on your schedule are more useful than standups you have to coordinate across time zones.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do Filipino developers work US business hours?**

Many do, particularly developers with prior international client experience. The practical arrangement that works well is agreeing on a specific daily overlap window before work starts — typically three to four hours where both parties are reliably reachable. That window covers calls, code reviews, and quick unblocks without requiring the developer to mirror US hours completely.

**Is it legally straightforward to engage a contractor from the Philippines?**

For a project-based or retainer freelance engagement, this is typically a services contract between two private parties, which is generally straightforward with a standard services agreement that includes IP assignment clauses. For full-time, ongoing work where you want benefits and compliance handled properly, an employer of record simplifies the legal picture considerably without requiring you to set up a Philippine entity.

**How do I pay a developer based in the Philippines?**

Wise (formerly TransferWise) handles international transfers with competitive exchange rates and low fees, and is widely used in this market. PayPal is familiar to most Filipino developers who work with international clients. Platforms like Upwork manage payment through escrow if you want additional structure for a first engagement. Direct bank wire is also an option, though it typically involves higher fees and slower settlement.

## The bottom line

Hiring a developer from the Philippines is a developer hire with some additional logistical decisions layered on top. The Philippines-specific factors — English as a working language, US-compatible time zone flexibility, and a well-developed pipeline of production web development experience — make it a strong market to search in. The failures come from anchoring on cost rather than capability, going through agencies that rotate staff without disclosure, or skipping the ownership and overlap conversations that protect you if the relationship does not work out.
